Job Market Paper

There Is Something in the AirRecovering Expectations from Public Interviews with Mutual Fund Managers

Solo authored

I measure individual U.S. mutual fund managers' expectations from television interviews during 2012–2025 and link them to the portfolios they manage. Within managers, greater stated optimism about the U.S. stock market is associated with increased market exposure through trading. Allocation funds shift toward U.S. equities, while equity funds shift toward higher-beta stocks. Funds also increase positions in industries and stocks their managers discuss favorably, with stock purchases continuing after the interview. Managers extrapolate past returns at every forecast horizon, yet the difference between long- and short-horizon sentiment is countercyclical and predicts subsequent increases in portfolio beta. Managers differ persistently in their optimism and responses to market conditions, and these differences are reflected in portfolio adjustments. Public interviews thus reveal subjective beliefs that correspond to how professional investors allocate capital.

Working Papers and Research in Progress

Agent-Based Trading and Price Discovery

with Svetlana Bryzgalova and Vicente Garcia

Equilibrium asset-pricing theory predicts that risk premia compensate investors for exposure to aggregate risk, yet how individual trading decisions bring prices into line with this prediction remains an open question. Field tests are difficult to interpret because the relevant beliefs, payoff distributions and market portfolio are only imperfectly observed. This paper investigates this question in controlled markets populated by LLM traders. The economy's payoffs, state probabilities and aggregate endowments are specified by design, allowing prices and allocations to be assessed against theoretical benchmarks. Agents observe their own portfolios and market activity but are not given aggregate endowments, allowing an examination of whether equilibrium pricing emerges through their interaction. The experiments vary agents' risk attitudes and trading approaches while holding payoffs and market rules fixed to examine how differences in behaviour shape pricing and risk sharing. The markets generate positive risk premia and patterns consistent with risk sharing, while exhibiting departures from the predicted ordering of state prices and from the CAPM prediction that the market portfolio is mean–variance efficient. The results distinguish the emergence of compensation for risk from the stronger requirement that risk be priced across assets as equilibrium theory predicts.

Do Market Prices Reflect Biased Information? Evidence from Voluntary Corporate Disclosures

with Marco Grotteria and Roberto Gómez-Cram

This paper investigates how financial markets assimilate potentially biased information into asset prices, particularly in the context of managerial announcements and subsequent analyst forecasts. By deploying a high-frequency identification strategy, the study empirically demonstrates that market prices do not entirely reflect the quantitative information provided by managers, even when it holds value-relevance. The analysis reveals that stock prices react similarly to initial managerial forecasts and subsequent, albeit slightly more optimistic, analyst repetitions. This persistence in market movement suggests that investors' reactions are not merely a product of news novelty but rather a heterogeneous interpretation of managerial bias. The paper challenges the gradual information-diffusion hypothesis, instead proposing a model where investor disagreement on managerial bias predictions leads to positive autocorrelation in stock returns. These findings contribute to the discourse on market efficiency, suggesting that the transparency of bias, rather than its mere presence, is instrumental in shaping market reactions to new information.

Symbolic Regressions: Opening the Black Box of Equity Premium Predictions

Predicting the aggregate equity premium remains a central challenge in asset pricing because standard financial predictors often fail out of sample. I use deep symbolic regression to identify parsimonious nonlinear models linking excess market returns to prices, dividends, earnings and risk measures. Simulations show that the method can recover the true functional form of the data-generating process with sufficient data and appropriate regularization. In monthly U.S. data from 1927 to 2021, a parsimonious model produces lower out-of-sample forecast errors than linear and tuned random-forest benchmarks. Greater complexity improves in-sample fit but generally weakens forecasts. Performance also varies across economic episodes: some models perform well during the Great Recession but poorly during the pandemic.
